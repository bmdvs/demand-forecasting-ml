Section 1

Executive Summary

The demand forecasting model sets the reorder decisions for each of the shop's 1,339 stocked items. The model takes the parts that booked jobs will draw as known and forecasts the rest of each item's usage before a new order could arrive, then turns the total into a reorder point and order quantity. It has been live for the past six months, from January through June 2026. Its reorder points and order quantities are loaded straight into the ERP's purchasing screen, where the buyers release the suggested orders.

Before the model, reordering was done manually and ran on data that was messy and couldn't be trusted. Much of the reordering process was labor-intensive and imprecise: stock levels and lead times were stale, stock on hand was unverified, safety stock was inflated, and rush orders were relied upon to compensate for shortfalls. The result was the shop carrying excess inventory, 161 days of usage, yet still logging elevated stockout days, held jobs for missing material and rush freight spend.

The results below compare January to June 2026 with 2025 as the shop recorded it, with 2025's two halves averaged to a six-month basis. Stockout days fell 36%, jobs held for material fell 43% and rush spend fell 46%, while average inventory fell 5%, to 148 days of usage from 161.

Over the same six months of demand, the model also beat the two ways the shop could have reordered without it: the status quo, as the shop ran in 2025, and manual reordering on the cleaned records. It cut stockout days, held jobs and rush spend the furthest of the three, and carried slightly more inventory than either.

Section 2

Model Overview

Section 2.1

What This Model Does

The demand forecasting model is built on an ensemble decision-tree algorithm called a random forest. Every week, the model predicts which items need to be reordered, and these predictions are then fed directly into the ERP for each of the 1,336 items it scores. The model's reorder decisions are based on the current stock on hand and on order for each item, the forecasted usage over each supplier's delivery time, and a safety buffer based on how unpredictable each item's demand and deliveries have been.

1. What it reads
Three years of cleaned usage history for every stocked item, with its demand pattern, value class, cost and supplier lead time, and the component requirements of booked jobs.
→
2. What it predicts
Every week, one forecast per item: how many units the shop will use before a new order placed today could arrive.
→
3. What it produces
For every item, when to reorder and how much, loaded into the ERP, which checks every item daily and flags what to order.

The model refreshes its forecasts every week and is retrained on the latest history once a month. The reorder point covers the usage the ERP cannot see ahead; the ERP nets the requirements of booked jobs itself. The model's predictions are loaded directly into the ERP, which flags when each item should be reordered and how much to order. To keep working capital low, the recommendations for how much to order are informed by the item's cost: expensive items are bought every few weeks in small lots, and cheap items a few times a year in larger lots.

The ERP's reorder queue ranks every stocked item against the model's weekly reorder point: items at or below it are marked "ORDER NOW", items within two weeks of it "ORDER SOON", and items more than two weeks outside of it "OK". Parts that have been replaced by a successor are marked "PHASE OUT", with the successor named, and carry no suggested order. Each line also shows the stock on hand, allocated to released jobs, booked over the lead time (the component requirements of booked jobs, which the model takes as known) and on order, the forecast, the safety stock and the suggested order quantity, as seen in the screenshot of the ERP system below:

Section 2.2

Training Data Overview

The model learns from the shop's historical usage of every stocked item. It was originally trained on three years of this usage data (2023 through 2025, as shown below), and is continually trained on every new month of data.

Demand is steady in aggregate over the three years, but individual items exhibit very different demand patterns. We've categorized these individual item demand patterns into four groups, which the model is calibrated against: 409 smooth (regular and steady), 423 erratic (regular but variable), 288 lumpy (irregular and variable) and 219 intermittent (many months with no demand at all). One representative item within each pattern is shown below.

Three years of monthly usage, one representative item per demand pattern

Part of each item's usage is known before it happens. On the 516 parts that sit on a bill of materials, the component requirements of booked jobs account for about a third (36%) of total usage over the item's lead time; floor pulls and service issues are the rest, and that remainder is what the model forecasts. Parts that sit on no bill are forecast in full.

Individual demand patterns are informed by the kind of item, though every category carries all four patterns. Smooth demand is most common among fasteners (50%) and consumables (44%), the floor stock used consistently every week. Erratic demand is spread widely, and is highest among raw material (35%) and hardware (35%). Lumpy demand concentrates in raw material (31%) and outside service (29%). Intermittent demand is highest in mechanical (30%) and electrical (20%), where just a few of these high-cost items are used for a job or sold as occasional spares.

Items in each category, by demand pattern

Item cost and usage move in opposite directions across the categories. Mechanical and electrical items (motors, gearboxes, drives, controls) are 36% of items and 57% of usage value, with median unit costs of $41 and $25, but only 12% of the units used. Fasteners and hardware are the reverse: 53% of the units used but 9% of the value, at median unit costs under $3. The shop's working capital is therefore tied up in a few hundred expensive, slower-moving items, not in the high-volume floor stock.

Section 3.1

Scoring Summary

Across the 26 Monday refreshes of the buyers' queue, it made 33,644 predictions: 1,275 (4%) were marked ORDER NOW and 3,151 (9%) ORDER SOON, and the rest were OK. In a typical week that is about 49 items to order now and 121 to order soon, a short, ranked list for the buyers to work through. On June 30, 2026 the queue held 38 items to order now, 150 to order soon and 33 to phase out: parts replaced by a successor, which the shop runs down rather than reorders.

Three candidate algorithms were tuned and compared on their forecasts for that year, and the random forest model, with the lowest error, was selected. Section 3.3

Assumptions & Limitations

  • It forecasts demand, not supply. The model predicts how much the shop will use; it takes each supplier's lead time from its recent deliveries, refreshed monthly, and does not predict a late delivery. The buffer covers the usual spread of deliveries, not a supplier failure.
  • Booking visibility. Customer orders are booked 28 to 56 days before their jobs are released.
  • Expediting is held constant. All three replays expedite the same items the purchasing manager tracked before go-live, so rush spend reflects how often those items were at risk, not a change in how hard the shop chases suppliers.
  • Intermittent items ran high in live use. After bias correction, the forecasts for intermittent items ran high in March and April (+27% and +24%), which the monitor flagged for retraining; the buffers on those items were larger than needed in those months.
